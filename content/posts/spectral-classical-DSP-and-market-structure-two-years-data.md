## Classical signal processing generate alpha without machine learning?

That was the question I set out to answer two years ago.

No LLMs. No deep learning. No training-test splits.

Just deterministic algorithms — spectral decomposition, multi-scale estimation, geometric and therodyanmics state-space modeling applied to market data.

**Here’s what I found — and why I think classical methods deserve more attention in quant finance.**

---

## The Constraint

Most quantitative strategies today rely on some form of machine learning. There's nothing wrong with that — but it comes with baggage:

- Overfitting risk
- Black-box interpretability
- Sensitivity to regime changes

I wanted to see how far pure signal processing could go. So I imposed a hard constraint:

**Every signal must come from a deterministic DSP pipeline. No data-driven training. No optimization against historical returns.**

The pipeline looks like this:

- **Multi-scale decomposition** — separate structural carriers from localized fluctuations
- **State-space estimation** — track latent system dynamics in real time
- **Adaptive parameter identification** — let the data speak through online updates, not batch fitting

The goal wasn't to beat machine learning. It was to see if a completely different approach — transparent, interpretable, deterministic — could stand on its own.

---

## The Results (June 2024 – June 2026)

I ran the framework across 17 US equities over a 2-year period (roughly 500 trading days). All returns were evaluated on a directional basis — long/short signals with time horizons determined by the model's internal estimates.

**Two critical transparency notes before the numbers:**

1. **No ML, no deep learning.** Every signal is generated by classical DSP algorithms. No training data was used. No fitting against historical returns occurred. This means zero overfitting risk by construction.

2. **No stop-losses, no take-profits, no dynamic position-sizing.** This is pure alpha validation — the system enters and exits purely on directional signals. Every trade runs its full predicted duration without execution-layer intervention. Drawdowns reflect raw signal performance.

## Three Things That Jumped Out

(Note: "Macro" and "Micro" here refer to internal signal layers — the primary state-estimation output vs. the auxiliary wavelet-based transient component — not macroeconomics or market microstructure.)

### 1. Micro-structure signals were worthless (for this framework)

I tested three signal variants:

- **Macro** — structural carrier analysis (spectral decomposition + state estimation)
- **Micro** — high-frequency transient analysis
- **Composite** — a combined version

Macro outperformed consistently. Micro produced negative Sharpe across almost every asset. Composite didn't add value — in many cases it degraded performance relative to pure Macro.

**Lesson**: The structural layer is doing the heavy lifting. The micro layer just adds noise.

### 2. Correlation with industry didn't matter

Tech, crypto-exposed, consumer, semis — the framework performed across all sectors. The best performers included a mix of growth (TSLA), tech (META), and even Chinese names (BABA, BIDU).

**Lesson**: This isn't a sector rotation strategy. It's capturing a cross-asset structural phenomenon.

### 3. Risk-free rate adjustment barely moved the needle

After deducting 4% (current US risk-free rate), the top performers stayed top performers. Only MSFT dropped below 1.0, and TSM fell to negative EV territory.

**Lesson**: The strategy generates genuine excess return — not just beta dressed up as alpha.

---

## What Didn't Work

Three clear failure modes emerged:

**1. Micro-only signals (auxiliary wavelet component)**
In my system, "Micro" refers to the auxiliary transient signal (wavelet-based local decomposition), not market microstructure. When evaluated independently as a directional source, it consistently underperformed the main state-estimation signal. This suggests its role should remain auxiliary — modulating entry timing or confidence, not generating primary direction.

**2. TSM**
Taiwan Semiconductor was the only asset with negative EV. The framework could not consistently trade it — suggesting either a structural issue with the asset itself or a weakness in the model's treatment of its particular dynamics.

---

## What This Means for Quant Strategy Design

The results suggest three principles:

**1. Start with structural models, not ML.**
A clean DSP pipeline can capture meaningful alpha without the overfitting baggage of machine learning. The signals are interpretable, stable, and reproducible.

**2. Test the micro layer separately.**
Micro model, if only based on localized space and time analysis, not configured properly with macro market structure dybamics, the value added is inconsistent and yields mostly noise.

**3. Build portfolios, not single-asset bets.**
Even with strong individual Sharpe ratios, some assets have large drawdowns. True stability comes from diversification — both across assets and across signal types.

## Next Steps

I'm extending this work in three directions:

1. **Portfolio-level testing** — constructing an equal-weighted Macro-only portfolio across the top 14 assets (excluding TSM, down-weighting MSFT and MSTR). Expect improved Sharpe and drawdown metrics.
2. **Risk overlay** — adding independent risk management modules (volatility targeting, trailing stops) without touching the core signal pipeline.
3. **Out-of-sample validation** — slicing the data by year to confirm stability across different market regimes.

## Final Thoughts

This experiment convinced me that **classical signal processing still has a place in quant finance.**

It doesn't produce the flashiest results. It won't generate headlines like "LLM predicts market direction." But it produces something more valuable in the long run:

- Fully interpretable decisions
- No overfitting by construction
- Stable cross-asset performance
- A clear path to risk management

Machine learning is powerful. But sometimes the signal was already there — you just needed the right filter to extract it.
